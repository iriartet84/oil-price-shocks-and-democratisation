# Oil Price Shocks and Democratization: Evidence from Regime-Dependent Effects
## Overview

This paper replicates and extends the empirical analysis of Brückner, Ciccone, and Tesei (2012) on the relationship between oil price shocks and political outcomes.

The replication asks whether the effect of oil price shocks on democratization is constant across countries, or whether it depends on the initial level of political institutions.

The analysis combines a replication of the original empirical results with an extension using endogenous threshold models and local projections to examine whether oil-price shocks have different effects across political regimes and whether positive and negative shocks produce asymmetric responses.

## Research Question

Do oil price shocks affect democratization differently depending on a country's initial political institutions?

The paper focuses on two related questions:

Does the relationship between oil price shocks and political change vary across levels of political institutions?
Are positive and negative oil-price shocks associated with symmetric political responses across different regimes?
Replication

The first part of the project reproduces the main empirical results of Brückner, Ciccone, and Tesei (2012) using their replication data.

The replication establishes a baseline against which the extensions can be evaluated and provides a direct check of the original empirical relationship between oil-price shocks and political outcomes.

## Extension: Regime-Dependent Effects

The main extension introduces an endogenous threshold framework in which the effect of oil-price shocks is allowed to vary according to a country's initial political institutions.

Rather than imposing a single average treatment effect across all countries, the model asks whether there is a threshold separating different political regimes.

The estimated threshold is approximately −6 on the Polity2 scale, although the confidence interval is wide, approximately [−8, 10]. This substantial uncertainty is important when interpreting the precise location of the regime boundary.

## Dynamic Effects

The paper also uses local projections to trace the dynamic response of political outcomes following oil-price shocks.

This makes it possible to examine not only whether political change occurs, but also how the response evolves over subsequent periods.

The analysis distinguishes between:

Positive oil-price shocks
Negative oil-price shocks
Different initial political regimes

This provides a more detailed picture of the political consequences of energy-market shocks than a single contemporaneous regression coefficient.

## Main Findings

The replication broadly reproduces the central empirical relationship documented in the original study.

The extension suggests that the response to oil-price shocks is regime-dependent.

Countries with relatively more open political institutions exhibit a positive association between positive oil-price shocks and subsequent changes in democracy, while the estimated effect is small and statistically weak below the estimated threshold.

The dynamic analysis also indicates asymmetry between positive and negative shocks. More authoritarian regimes show little positive response to oil-price booms but substantially stronger democratic declines following negative oil-price shocks. More open regimes display more muted responses.

These results suggest that the political consequences of energy shocks cannot necessarily be described by a single effect applying uniformly across political systems.

The relatively wide confidence interval around the threshold also means that the precise institutional boundary should not be interpreted too literally.

## Methodology

The empirical analysis combines:

Replication of the original baseline specifications
Endogenous threshold estimation
Local projections
Regime-dependent specifications
Positive and negative oil-shock decompositions
Dynamic impulse-response analysis

The threshold framework allows the relationship between oil shocks and political outcomes to vary endogenously with initial political institutions rather than imposing an arbitrary regime classification.
